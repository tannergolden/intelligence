# Strategy toolkit - the frameworks for strategize mode

*A library of strategy frameworks for the strategize mode, grouped by the job each does. The chair picks the few the planning cycle needs, names them in the brief, and never runs all of them.*

This is the toolkit the CEO (the chair) reaches into when the run is **strategize**: set or refresh the company thesis and the OKRs, pick the framework the question actually needs, and write `Strategy.md` and the `Goals.md` the OKRs ladder up to; or, for the Founder, set their life strategy and their goals for the year (Group F). It is distinct from `frameworks.md`, which is the **decision** toolkit (dissent, decision rights, scoring, future-testing) for a single consequential call in **decide** mode. Strategy frameworks tell you *where to play and how to win*; decision frameworks tell you *how to choose well among framed options*. A strategize run usually produces or refreshes a strategy; a decide run resolves one choice. They compose: the diagnosis below frames the options a decide run then chooses among.

Each entry: a one-line what-it-is, its originator, and when to reach for it. Stage-scale every choice (`references/lifecycle.md`): a pre-revenue **found** venture diagnoses lightly and positions sharply on one segment; a **scale** venture leans on growth-choice and execution frameworks; a **maintain** venture leans on portfolio and horizon allocation to defend the core while funding the next S-curve.

## The sequence (diagnose before you choose)

Strategy has an order, and skipping it is the most common failure. **Diagnose** the environment and your position first, **position** the company within it, **choose** the growth vector second, and only then **execute** with a model and a scorecard. A growth bet (Ansoff, BCG) made before the diagnosis (Five Forces, value chain) is a guess dressed as a plan. Reach into the groups in order; on a quick refresh you may touch only one, but never let a later group overrule a diagnosis you never ran.

```
A. DIAGNOSE  ->  B. POSITION  ->  C. CHOOSE growth  ->  D. EXECUTE
(where are we)   (how we win)     (which vector)        (the model + the scoreboard)
                         \                                    /
                          E. PORTFOLIO (across ventures and projects, when more than one competes)

F. THE FOUNDER'S OWN LIFE (the same sequence, one level down: where I stand, what matters, the few goals, the weekly review)
```

## A. Diagnose - read the environment and your position

Run this group first. The output is a written situation: the forces acting on the venture, the rivalry it faces, where it actually makes money, and the honest internal-vs-external picture. It frames everything downstream.

- **PESTEL.** A scan of the macro-environment across six external forces - Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal - that the venture does not control but must navigate. Popularized from Francis Aguilar's 1967 ETPS scan. Reach for it when entering a new market or geography, at the start of an annual cycle, or whenever a regulatory, technological, or macro shift could reset the board. It feeds the "why now" of a thesis and the threats line of a SWOT.
- **Porter's Five Forces.** A structural read of an industry's profit potential through five forces - rivalry among existing competitors, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, bargaining power of suppliers. Michael Porter, HBR 1979. Reach for it to judge whether a market is structurally attractive before entering, to find where your margin is being competed away, or to locate the force you can blunt (raise an entry barrier, reduce buyer power). It diagnoses the industry; it does not tell you your position within it - that is the value chain and SWOT.
- **SWOT.** The honest internal-vs-external snapshot: Strengths and Weaknesses (internal, things you control) against Opportunities and Threats (external, things you do not). Roots at Stanford/SRI in the 1960s. Reach for it as the synthesis step after PESTEL and Five Forces, to converge a diagnosis onto a single page before positioning. Caveat: a SWOT run cold, with no PESTEL or Five Forces feeding it, degrades into an unranked list of opinions; feed it real external analysis and force-rank each quadrant, or it is theatre.
- **Value chain analysis.** Decompose the firm into the primary and support activities that create value (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service; plus procurement, technology, HR, infrastructure) to locate where cost and differentiation actually originate. Michael Porter, 1985. Reach for it to find your real source of advantage or your cost problem - which activity to own, which to outsource, where margin leaks. It is the internal complement to Five Forces' external read, and it grounds the cost-leadership vs differentiation choice in B.

## B. Position - choose how you win

With the diagnosis in hand, decide the basis of advantage and the customer you serve. The output is a positioning statement the OKRs can ladder up to.

- **Porter's generic strategies.** Three mutually-constraining ways to win - cost leadership (be the low-cost producer), differentiation (be meaningfully distinct and command a premium), or focus (dominate a narrow segment on either basis). Michael Porter, 1980. Reach for it to force the foundational choice and to avoid being "stuck in the middle" (Porter's term for a firm that is neither cheapest nor distinct and therefore wins on nothing). A venture that cannot say which of the three it is has not chosen a strategy.
- **Blue Ocean Strategy.** Stop competing in a bloody "red ocean" of existing demand and create uncontested "blue ocean" market space by changing the value-cost frontier - raise and create what the industry undervalues, reduce and eliminate what it over-serves (the Four Actions / ERRC grid; the strategy canvas as its picture). W. Chan Kim and Renee Mauborgne, 2005. Reach for it when the market is commoditized and a head-to-head fight on price or features is unwinnable, and you want a non-obvious move that makes the competition irrelevant rather than beaten.
- **Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD).** Customers "hire" a product to make progress on a job in their life; define the market by the job, not the product category or the demographic. Clayton Christensen (with Anthony Ulwick's outcome-driven variant). Reach for it to find the real competitive set (the job's other solutions, including "do nothing"), to spot under-served outcomes, and to anchor positioning and the roadmap in progress the customer is trying to make. The sharpest input to a found-stage venture's positioning and to the market requirements a product decision rests on (the research-and-development skill gathers the evidence; the CEO decides).

## C. Choose the growth vector

Positioning settled, pick how to grow. These frameworks choose *direction*; they presume the diagnosis already told you the terrain.

- **Ansoff Matrix.** Four growth vectors by risk - market penetration (existing products, existing markets; lowest risk), market development (existing products, new markets), product development (new products, existing markets), and diversification (new products, new markets; highest risk). Igor Ansoff, HBR 1957. Reach for it to name the growth move explicitly and to weigh its risk, so a "let's expand" impulse becomes a chosen, risk-rated vector rather than four bets at once.
- **BCG growth-share matrix.** Plot each product or business on market growth (vertical) against relative market share (horizontal) into Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks, and Dogs, to guide where to invest, hold, harvest, or divest. Bruce Henderson, Boston Consulting Group, 1970. Reach for it for a fast, vivid portfolio read of a multi-product venture. Caveat: it reduces the world to two axes and assumes share drives cash; treat it as a conversation-starter, not a verdict, and prefer the GE matrix when you need nuance.
- **GE-McKinsey nine-box matrix.** The richer cousin of BCG: plot business units on industry attractiveness against competitive strength, each a composite of several weighted factors, into a 3x3 grid that signals invest/grow, hold/selective, or harvest/divest. McKinsey for General Electric, early 1970s. Reach for it when the BCG two-axis read is too blunt - when "market growth" and "share" alone mislead, and you need multi-factor attractiveness and strength to allocate across units.
- **Three Horizons.** Allocate effort and capital across three horizons at once - H1 defend and extend the core business, H2 build emerging growth engines, H3 seed genuinely new options - so today's earnings and tomorrow's S-curve are funded in parallel. McKinsey (Baghai, Coley, White, 1999). Reach for it whenever the planning cycle must balance present and future, especially in **maintain** stage where the danger is starving H2/H3 to optimize H1. The default split moves by stage (`references/lifecycle.md`): roughly 100/0/0 at **found**, 70/20/10 at **scale** and **maintain**.

## D. Execute - turn the choice into a model and a scoreboard

A chosen strategy that is not modeled and measured is a wish. These frameworks make it operational and connect it to the OKRs.

- **Business Model Canvas.** A one-page map of how the business creates, delivers, and captures value across nine blocks - customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships, cost structure. Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, 2010. Reach for it to make an existing or proposed model explicit and stress-test its coherence (do the pieces fit), and as the executable layer beneath a positioning choice.
- **Lean Canvas.** Ash Maurya's 2010 adaptation of the Business Model Canvas for early-stage ventures: it swaps four blocks for problem, solution, key metrics, and unfair advantage, foregrounding the riskiest unknowns. Reach for it in **found** stage and in the founding playbook - it is the canvas that fits a pre-PMF venture, where the job is to find the riskiest assumption, not to map a settled model. See `references/founding-playbook.md`.
- **Balanced Scorecard.** Translate strategy into measurable objectives across four linked perspectives - financial, customer, internal process, and learning and growth - so the scoreboard reflects more than this quarter's revenue. Kaplan and Norton, HBR 1992. Reach for it to connect the strategy to a balanced metric set and to keep leading indicators (learning, process) visible beside lagging financials; it is the natural bridge from `Strategy.md` into `Goals.md` and the metrics in `references/metrics-and-kpis.md`.

## E. Portfolio - strategy across ventures and projects

When more than one venture exists, or several projects compete for the same capital, time or attention (the Founder's personal projects among them), strategy is also an allocation problem across them, not only within each. This is the cross-venture deep-tier case (`references/dispatch.md`); convene it only when the question genuinely spans ventures or projects. The Chief Operating Officer weighs the portfolio and recommends the mix; the CEO decides a reversible allocation within the company's mandate, and the Founder decides one that moves capital between ventures or spends their own time and money.

- **Reuse the portfolio frameworks from C, one level up.** BCG and especially the GE-McKinsey nine-box generalize cleanly from products-within-a-venture to ventures-within-a-portfolio: plot each venture on attractiveness against strength to see where to invest, hold, or wind down. **Three Horizons** is the portfolio's backbone - across ventures it is the explicit H1/H2/H3 split of capital and the Founder's attention, so a mature cash venture funds an emerging one without starving it.
- **Weigh, do not just sort.** A matrix sorts; it does not rank. When the call is "which venture, initiative or project gets the next increment of scarce capital or attention," the Chief Operating Officer scores the contenders explicitly by RICE (Reach times Impact times Confidence divided by Effort, comparable across ventures where a score with an "ease" term is not), or a weighted matrix (see `frameworks.md` for the Pugh method). The matrix frames the field, the score ranks it, and the chair names one accountable owner for the allocation.
- **Balance outranks raw score.** Flag over-concentration when any one venture, project or theme takes more than roughly 60 percent of the total effort, treat a bottom-tier score as a cut candidate unless a stated strategic priority lifts it, and name that priority whenever it overrides the score. A field with only one venture's or one project's contenders is not a portfolio: rank it on merit (the research-and-development skill's ★ Head of Research & Development runs a pairwise tournament) instead of balancing it.
- **Hold the standard.** Portfolio calls are where every angle must be heard and one owner must be accountable: each venture's chief argues its own case in isolation (no venture grades its own claim to capital), the chair alone holds the cross-venture synthesis, and capital movements are gated to the Founder (`references/write-gate.md`). Portfolio allocation moves real money or equity; it is never an autonomous write.

## F. The Founder's own life - life strategy and the year's goals

The same sequence runs one level down when the strategize run is the Founder's own: their life strategy, and the goals and priorities for the year and the quarter. The chair frames it from what the Founder says matters (and the profile the human-resources skill keeps, where a private repository holds it); the departments whose personal scope a goal touches are heard; the Founder decides every goal. The result is personal context, written only where a private repository made to hold it allows, and otherwise returned in the conversation.

- **Diagnose: where the Founder stands.** A short, honest read across the areas of life the departments cover (money, work and career, health and wellbeing habits, household and routines, learning and side projects, relationships and reputation), each in a line: what is working, what is not, what is changing. The Wheel of Life (commonly credited to Paul J. Meyer) is a quick picture of the same read; use it to see the balance, not to score a life.
- **Position: what matters most.** The few values and the one or two areas that will get the year's energy, and, as plainly, what the Founder will say no to. A year that tries to move every area moves none.
- **Choose: the few goals.** Three to five goals for the year, each with a measure and a date, set as personal OKRs (`templates/okr-cycle.md`, its personal note), committed or aspirational by the same rule as a company's.
- **Execute: the quarter and the week.** Each quarter's key results ladder to the year's goals; each week's priorities ladder to the quarter's, sorted by importance before urgency (the Eisenhower matrix, popularized by Stephen Covey in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, 1989), and checked in the weekly review (`operating-cadence.md`, the Founder's own cadence).

A big choice that surfaces while setting the goals (a move, a career change, a purchase that reshapes the budget) is not settled inside the strategize run: it becomes its own decide run, with the departments it touches heard and the Founder deciding.

## Picking a framework

Match the tool to the job, and respect the sequence. If you do not yet know the terrain, start in Group A (diagnose) - PESTEL and Five Forces for the outside, value chain and SWOT to converge it. Once the diagnosis is written, settle the basis of advantage in Group B (generic strategies to choose cost vs differentiation vs focus; Blue Ocean when the fight is unwinnable head-on; JTBD to anchor on the customer's real job). Then choose the growth vector in Group C (Ansoff to name the move, BCG or the GE matrix to read the portfolio, Three Horizons to balance now against next), and make it operational in Group D (a canvas for the model, the Balanced Scorecard to wire it to the OKRs). Go to Group E only when the question spans ventures or competing projects, and to Group F when the plan is the Founder's own life.

A quick refresh may touch a single framework (re-grade Three Horizons, redraw one Lean Canvas block). A standard strategize run diagnoses with two Group A tools, settles one Group B choice, picks one Group C vector, and lands it in a Balanced Scorecard that feeds `Goals.md`. A deep run, a market-entry or a full annual plan, walks all four groups in order and writes the result into `Strategy.md` behind the write-gate. Whatever the tier: diagnose before you choose, name the frameworks in the brief, run only the few the question needs, and resolve to one thesis with one accountable owner.
