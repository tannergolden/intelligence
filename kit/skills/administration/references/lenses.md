# Lenses - what each department brings

The distinct view each department lead holds on a cross-department decision, and how to brief each one to get a sharp view rather than mush. The chair convenes the lenses the decision actually touches, never all of them by reflex. Each lead is dispatched from its own department skill's `agents/` (`workforce.md`) on the same framed question and the same criteria, and returns an independent written view: its recommendation, its assumptions, and the tradeoffs its department sees.

Cross-functional decisions are better because the structure "puts every needed perspective in the same room, so a decision that would normally take three rounds of email gets made once" (Burrus, "Cross-Functional Team Leadership"). The value comes from the lenses being genuinely different, so protect the difference.

## The department lenses

- **Finance & Accounting (the finance-and-accounting skill's ★ Chief Financial Officer): returns, risk, cash.** Owns the ROI and payback case, the capital and cash-flow impact, and the downside scenarios. Note the documented bias: finance leaders tend to speak up "only if their business areas were being discussed," so the chair must explicitly draw the lens onto the whole decision, not just its budget line (McKinsey, "Untangling Your Organization's Decision Making").
- **Legal & Compliance (the legal-and-compliance skill's ★ General Counsel): liability and compliance.** Holds veto-grade input on legal and regulatory thresholds, the Agree role in RAPID (see `synthesis.md`), not a general opinion. Frame the ask as "what would make this impermissible or materially risky," not "what do you think."
- **Marketing (the sales-and-marketing skill's ★ Chief Revenue Officer, with its Marketing Manager producing): customer and brand.** Voice of the customer, brand and reputational fit, the demand response. The lens on whether the market will want and reward the move (brand, demand, customer). The Marketing Manager sits on the Sales & Marketing team; the brand-voice consistency gate sits with the public-relations skill's ★ Chief Communications Officer.
- **Sales (the sales-and-marketing skill's ★ Chief Revenue Officer): the revenue number.** The pipeline, the forecast, the close, and the single revenue number across marketing, sales, and customer experience. A distinct lens from marketing: where the marketing lens asks whether the market will want the move, the revenue lens asks what it does to bookings, win rate, and the quota-carrying motion. Both feed the sales records through distinct files.
- **Operations & Logistics (the operations-and-logistics skill's ★ Head of Operations): feasibility and capacity.** Can the move actually be delivered, at what scale, on what timeline, with what constraints. The lens that turns a good idea into a deliverable one.
- **Human Resources (the human-resources skill's ★ Chief Human Resources Officer): people and culture.** Headcount, the change-management load, morale, and cultural fit. The lens on whether the organization can absorb the move.
- **Information Technology (the information-technology skill's ★ Chief Information Officer): systems and security.** Integration cost, data security, technical risk, and what the internal systems can and cannot support. The IT team's CISO carries the security-risk line straight to the Founder (`workforce.md`).
- **Business Intelligence (the business-intelligence skill's ★ Chief Data Officer): the evidence in the company's numbers.** The data that grounds or undercuts the case; the lens that replaces opinion with measurement where measurement exists.
- **Research & Development (the research-and-development skill's ★ Head of Research & Development): evidence, options, and the build.** The evidence about users and markets (what is validated and what is not), the options when the choice set is thin, and technical feasibility, innovation risk, and what the move does to the build. The product call itself is the chair's: the CEO weighs these and decides what to build.
- **Customer Experience (the customer-experience skill's ★ Chief Customer Officer): the lived experience.** What the move does to existing customers, retention, and the support load.
- **Quality Assurance (the quality-assurance skill's ★ Head of Quality): the standard.** Whether the move holds the quality bar, and where it risks breaking it. Its Devil's Advocate is the dissent role, not a lens: it argues against the front-runner once the views are in (`dispatch.md`).
- **Public Relations (the public-relations skill's ★ Chief Communications Officer): reputation.** How the move reads externally and internally, and the communications risk.

## The lenses on a personal decision

A life choice convenes the same leads, each bringing its department's personal scope (`workforce.md`, the department table), and each briefed on the Founder's own goals and constraints rather than a company's:

- **Finance & Accounting:** what the choice costs and earns over time, its tax effect, the cash it needs, and what it does to the Founder's goals for money (an offer's total pay against the cost of living; a purchase against the budget and the debt).
- **Legal & Compliance:** the contract, the lease, the non-compete or the warranty the choice binds the Founder to, and where a licensed lawyer must review.
- **Sales & Marketing:** what is negotiable and how (the pay, the start date, the price), and what the choice does to the Founder's network and personal brand.
- **Operations & Logistics:** what it takes to carry the choice out (the move, the household, the commute, the contractors) and the timeline it can be done on.
- **Human Resources:** what it does to the Founder's career and skills, and to their health and wellbeing habits, against the profile where a private repository holds it.
- **Research & Development:** the facts the choice turns on (the city, the employer, the market for the thing bought), found and cited.

The other departments join when the choice touches them: information-technology for the devices and accounts a move or a new job changes, customer-experience for a purchase's return and warranty terms, public-relations for what the Founder will say and to whom. The Founder decides; the lenses inform.

## Briefing for sharpness, not mush

Give each lead the same decision frame and the same criteria, then ask for an independent written view before any group discussion. Require each department to state its recommendation and its assumptions, since surfacing assumptions is where structured methods add the most value (Schweiger, Sandberg and Ragan, Academy of Management Journal). Amazon's written-memo discipline exists for exactly this reason: "the best ideas come from diverse thinking, but as instinctive people-pleasers, it is hard to voice your opinion if it is different from that of the boss" (the Amazon meeting practice). A view written in isolation is harder to anchor than one spoken into a room that has already begun to lean.

## Convene only the lenses the decision touches

A pricing change is finance, marketing, sales, and legal, not HR and IT. A reorganization is HR, operations, and finance. A new data feature is IT, legal, research and development, and BI. A job offer in another city is finance, human resources, sales (the negotiation), and operations (the move). The chair names the relevant lenses in the frame and leaves the rest out; convening every department on a three-department question is wasted tokens and false weight. When unsure whether a lens matters, the chair decides during framing rather than convening it by reflex.
