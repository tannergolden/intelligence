# Difficult messages - the per-situation playbook

The hard correspondence: bad news, an apology, a refusal, a complaint response, a de-escalation. Each is a known shape with a known set of moves and a known set of ways to get it wrong. The lead names the message type in the situation read (`references/intake.md`); the Writer loads this reference when the situation is a hard message and drafts to the matching section. The cross-cutting principle runs through all five: **lead with the reader's frame, not yours.** Buffer, validate, or acknowledge before the hard content; state the hard thing clearly enough that it cannot be missed; always leave a path forward. The calm-warm-firm register from the tone-calibration rule carries every one of these.

## Bad news

**Situation.** An unwelcome decision, denial, delay, or disappointing outcome: a rejection, a policy denial, a cancellation, a price increase.

**Structure (indirect, the default for serious or relationship-sensitive news).** Buffer, then reasons, then the news, then a goodwill close. Open with a neutral, truthful, relevant statement that sets a calm tone without alluding to the bad news or raising false hope. Give the reasons before the news, so the decision reads as a reasonable conclusion rather than an arbitrary verdict. State the news plainly enough that it cannot be missed, then de-emphasize it (a subordinate position, paired immediately with any alternative or workaround). Close forward-looking, without rehashing the news or inviting an argument. Use the **direct** form (news first, then reasons, then close) when the news is minor, the reader prefers directness, space is tight, an earlier indirect attempt failed, or the reader might skim past a buried point.

**Key moves and pitfalls.** State the actual outcome clearly despite the cushioning; keep the reasons reader-centered. Never raise expectations of good news in the buffer (deceptive), never bury the news so deep the reader misses it, and never pile on negative language or criticize the reader. **Never admit or imply fault or liability** without authorization cleared by legal counsel.

## Apology

**Situation.** Acknowledging and repairing an error or harm the writer's side caused.

**Structure.** The effective apology has up to six components (the more present, the more effective): an expression of regret, an explanation of what went wrong, an acknowledgment of responsibility, a declaration that it will not recur, an offer of repair, and a request for forgiveness. **Lead with the acknowledgment of responsibility** ("it is my fault, we made a mistake"); it is the single most load-bearing component, and the offer of repair is second. The strongest minimal bundle is responsibility plus repair plus explanation; the part to drop first, if any, is the request for forgiveness.

**Key moves and pitfalls.** Own the fault unconditionally and pair the regret with a real remedy and a commitment to change. Never use a conditional **"if" apology** ("sorry if I offended you," "if you were offended"): the conditional makes the harm hypothetical and voids the apology. Never blame-shift ("sorry you feel that way"), and never offer vague regret with no ownership and no repair ("mistakes were made").

## Refusal

**Situation.** Declining a request, invitation, or ask while keeping the relationship intact.

**Structure (the positive no, yes-no-yes).** First, **yes to a value**: ground the no in the underlying interest, need, or priority it protects ("stand on your feet without standing on their toes"). Then the **no to the request**: a clear, matter-of-fact refusal stated as an action and a limit, not a hostile reaction or a judgment of the asker. Then **yes to a path forward**: an alternative, compromise, or next step that respects both parties and keeps the door open.

**Key moves and pitfalls.** Anchor the no in the genuine yes-to-a-value first, so the refusal reads as principled rather than rejecting, then close with something workable. Make the no unambiguous. Never go aggressive, and never go so apologetic that the no wavers; never over-explain or pad with excuses that invite renegotiation; never leave the reader with only a closed door and no path.

## Complaint and service recovery

**Situation.** Replying to an upset customer or counterpart whose problem the writer's side must resolve.

**Structure.** Listen, empathize, own, resolve, follow up. Let them state the problem fully and mirror the specifics back so they feel heard. Name and validate the feeling. Apologize and take ownership on behalf of the organization, even if you did not personally cause it. Resolve with a fix fitted to their actual need, with concrete next steps and timing. Follow up after the fix to confirm satisfaction and address the root cause.

**Key moves and pitfalls.** **Acknowledge and empathize before resolving**: being heard is the customer's foremost goal, and jumping to the fix first reads as dismissive. A fast, owned fix plus a follow-up is what converts a complaint into retained loyalty (a well-handled failure can leave a customer more loyal than if nothing had gone wrong). Never defend, deflect blame onto the customer, or hide behind "per our policy"; never offer a generic fix before acknowledging the harm; never resolve and then never follow up.

## De-escalation

**Situation.** Lowering the temperature with someone already angry, in a channel where tone is easy to misread.

**Structure.** Validate, then correct. Open by validating the specific frustration in one human sentence, before any explanation or rebuttal. Own what is ownable without over-apologizing (a plain acknowledgment, not a defensive justification). Correct or inform only after the reader feels heard, in short warm sentences. Give clear next steps: exactly what you are doing and how long it takes.

**Key moves and pitfalls.** **Validate before you correct.** Emotion is contagious, so a calm, acknowledging open lowers the reader's temperature and earns the right to be heard, while a defensive or "you're wrong" open escalates. Match a calm tone, keep sentences short and free of corporate filler, and let them feel heard before steering to the solution. Never get defensive, blame the reader, or correct facts before acknowledging the feeling; never hide behind stock phrases ("unfortunately," "per our policy") that read as cold; never rush to the fix and skip the acknowledgment.
