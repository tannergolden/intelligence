# Intake - understand the situation before drafting

The first move on any external piece is not to write. It is to understand the situation the piece must fit, then draft to that situation rather than improvise it. A message is a fitting response to a real set of circumstances (a goal, a reader, a relationship, an emotional register, a set of constraints); the situation comes first and calls the writing into being. Get the situation wrong and a clean draft still misses. This reference is the understand-first method: the situation read, the tone-calibration rule, the elicitation rule, and the situation-brief spine the lead fills before the first drafting dispatch.

Intake is the **lead's** job (Phase 0), not a new team seat. The lead already holds the brief and synthesizes; running the situation read and writing the situation brief is a formalized lead responsibility. The brief then threads into every downstream dispatch so the Writer drafts to it and the Line Editor gates against it. The scaffold the lead fills is `templates/situation-brief.md`.

## The situation read

Two passes over the user's request and over any incoming message being answered: the rhetorical pass (what the piece must do, for whom, under what limits) and the sentiment pass (the emotional register the piece must meet). Run both before drafting.

### The rhetorical pass (the checks)

- **Goal / exigence.** Why must this message exist, and what must be different after the reader reads it? Name the change: a decision granted, an error corrected, an action prompted, a belief shifted, a relationship repaired. If nothing must change, do not send. This is the organizing principle of the whole piece.
- **Audience, relationship, and power.** Who is the one reader who can both be persuaded and actually act? Address them, not the cc line. Read what they already know (set the background and jargon to match), their likely attitude (receptive, neutral, resistant) and top objections, and the relationship's direction and closeness: upward (deferential request, not demand), downward (authority, not permissive), lateral (peer to peer). The power relationship sets the register.
- **The one key message.** The single thing the reader must take away, in one sentence. Lead with it. If you cannot state it in a sentence, the piece is not yet scoped.
- **Constraints.** The box the message lives in: deadline and timing, length and format expectations, what the reader already believes, the channel's norms, any policy or brand or legal rule, and how much standing the writer has to make the ask. Write within it.

The minimum viable read is the triad: goal (why / what changes), audience (who, what they know, how they feel), and context (relationship, medium, constraints). The four checks above expand it.

### The sentiment pass (read the emotional register)

The reader (and the user) has no face or voice on the page, so emotion is carried entirely by **clustered cues**: word choice, directness, hedging, punctuation, capitalization, sentence length, salutation, and sign-off. Scan them together and name a register before drafting. Read the incoming message AND the user's own request, which carries its own stress level and target register.

- **Word choice.** Absolutes ("always," "never," "unacceptable"), blame, and sarcasm read as anger; "ASAP," "urgent," "still waiting," and repeated questions read as anxiety or urgency; "thanks so much," first names, and "no rush" read as warmth; "per my last email," "kindly," and "please advise" read as formal or transactional.
- **Directness and hedging.** Blunt demands and accusations read as anger; rapid-fire or repeated asks read as chasing; heavy hedging ("maybe," "just checking") reads as anxiety; minimal and businesslike reads as transactional.
- **Punctuation and capitalization.** "?!" and "!!!" read as anger; a hard period on a short line ("Fine.") reads as final and abrupt; a question closed with a period ("Are you coming.") reads as a demand; trailing "..." reads as anxious; ALL CAPS reads as shouting. Clean single terminal marks read as neutral.
- **Sentence length.** Clipped one-liners read as terse or angry; long anxious run-ons or staccato bursts read as stress; medium and relaxed reads as warm; even and measured reads as formal.
- **Salutation and sign-off.** No greeting or just the name reads as cold or angry; "Hi [name]!" or "Hey" reads as warm; "Dear," title plus surname reads as formal. A curt or absent sign-off ("-J," or none) reads as annoyance or haste; "Thanks again!" or "Warmly" reads as warm; "Best regards," "Sincerely" reads as formal.

> [!WARNING]
> **Guard against negativity bias.**
> When tone cues are missing, imagination fills the blank, and it rarely fills it with good intent: a neutral message is easy to misread as cold or hostile, and people remember a message's emotional tone longer than its content. Weigh **clusters** of cues, never a single mark. One terse line from a busy sender is not anger. Read deliberately and do not assume hostility that is not there.

## The tone-calibration rule

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Mirror the register, never the emotion.**
> **Converge** on the other party's **warmth and formality** (matching their register builds rapport and signals respect). **Diverge** from their **anger, panic, and aggression**: hold a calm, warm, firm register instead. Matching frustration is the single worst move. Coldness reads as dislike and also escalates, so hold warmth steady and high against hostility rather than going cold. **Firmness scales with the stakes and the boundary of the situation, not with the sender's volume.** Calm, warm, and firm-on-substance beats matched heat every time.

| Their register | Converge (match it) | Diverge / hold steady (do NOT match) |
|---|---|---|
| Warm, friendly | warmth, first names, light tone | |
| Formal, businesslike | formality, structure, sign-off | |
| Angry, hostile | | hold calm, warm, firm; never match the heat |
| Anxious, panicked | | hold steady, reassuring, slower pace |
| Curt, transactional | match the brevity and efficiency | do not read it as anger and over-warm it |

For a charged reply where emotion is high and the relationship matters (a complaint, pushback, declining a request, correcting a mistake), structure the substance with the **NVC observation-feeling-need-request frame**: state the concrete observation without judgment (no "always / never"), name the actual feeling, connect it to the underlying need or value, and close with a specific, positive, doable request that stays open to "no." The frame sets the structure; the calm-warm-firm register sets the tone it is delivered in. Acknowledge and validate the reader's state in the first sentence, then address the substance. For the per-situation playbook on hard messages, see `references/difficult-messages.md`.

## The elicitation rule

The user wants help, not a questionnaire. Asking is the exception.

> [!TIP]
> **Ask at most 1 to 3 questions, and only when it changes the draft.**
> Ask only when **a different plausible answer would produce a materially different draft** AND **a wrong guess is costly to undo** (a sensitive recipient, a firing, a legal or money commitment, an unknown power dynamic). Otherwise draft now on explicitly stated, correctable assumptions.

- **Ask only what changes the draft.** If every reasonable answer yields about the same draft, do not ask. Assume and proceed.
- **Cap at the top 1 to 3, usually fewer (often zero).** Prioritize in this order, stopping as soon as the draft is determined: goal, then audience and relationship, then the one key message. Tone, format, and length are rarely worth a question; infer them from channel and relationship.
- **Batch once, never drip.** If you must ask, ask the 1 to 3 questions together, once, then draft. Never trickle one at a time, and never end an otherwise-complete draft with a list of questions the user did not request.
- **Otherwise draft and surface the assumptions.** When not asking, draft anyway and state the assumptions that drove it ("I assumed this is to your manager, the goal is X, and kept it brief; tell me if any of those are off"). A concrete artifact to react to is faster and more accurate than questions in the abstract, and every assumption stays visible and correctable.
- **Let memory pre-empt the question.** Before asking about audience or voice, check `Profile/` and `Memory/` for an existing voice profile or recipient and relationship record and fill from it. Re-asking known facts is a failure mode.
- **Use the structured ask tool** when it is available; otherwise ask in chat.

## The situation-brief spine

The fields the lead captures and threads into every dispatch. Each carries a sensible default so the lead can proceed when the user is terse; an unstated field gets its default, not a question. The scaffold is `templates/situation-brief.md`.

| Field | Captures | Default if unstated |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal / desired outcome** | what the reader should think, feel, or do (the exigence and any call to action) | infer from the artifact type; assume one modest outcome |
| **Audience and relationship** | who receives it, what they know, the direction and closeness, the power and formality | nearest plausible single reader; neutral-professional distance |
| **The one key message** | the single sentence the reader must take away | derive it from the request; lead with it |
| **Situation and stakes** | what happened, the history, what is at risk, the timing | treat as a standalone reply to the stated situation |
| **Sentiment read and tone calibration** | the register read from the cues, and the converge / diverge call it sets | neutral; converge on warmth and formality, hold calm-warm-firm under heat |
| **Register** | the Joos rung and the warmth-versus-firmness setting | match the channel and relationship; consultative by default |
| **Medium and destination format** | email, message platform, plain text, or document, which picks the format reference | infer from the request; default to the channel's norm |
| **Constraints** | length, deadline, must-include, must-avoid, policy or legal or brand rules | fit the medium's norms; keep it concise; assume no must-haves |
| **Message type** (if a hard message) | bad news, apology, refusal, complaint and recovery, or de-escalation | none; set only when the situation is a hard message, then load `references/difficult-messages.md` |

Load the voice into the brief from `Profile/` per `references/voice.md`: pass the concrete captured features, or the honest neutral-default note when `Profile/` is empty (do not invent a voice). The situation brief plus the loaded voice is what every Writer and Line Editor dispatch carries.
