# Synthesis - combining conflicting views into one call

How the chair turns several conflicting functional views into one recommendation with one accountable owner, without letting the loudest function, the highest-paid opinion, or a premature consensus decide. Read before the decide phase. Only the chair holds every view plus the Founder's real question, so the chair alone synthesizes; the functions advise.

## One decider, one owner (decision rights)

Use an explicit decision-rights frame so disagreement resolves cleanly. Bain's RAPID insists on a single Decider: there should ideally be only one decision-maker per choice. It separates a narrow veto (Agree, for example legal or risk) from non-binding Input, and leaves no doubt about who is on point (Bain, "RAPID"). The D is the formal decision maker who is ultimately responsible and has the authority to resolve any impasse, with veto reserved for the few (Rogers and Blenko, HBR, "Who Has the D?"). McKinsey frames the same rule as a single point of accountability: every decision has exactly one owner accountable for its quality, timeliness, and execution (McKinsey, "Untangling Your Organization's Decision Making"). This prevents the twin failures of everyone-decides (paralysis) and no-one-decides (drift).

In this skill the Decider is the CEO for reversible, in-mandate calls, and the Founder for irreversible or major ones. The recommendation always names one accountable owner for the follow-through, distinct from the decider: the decider commits the call, the owner carries it out.

## Beating the highest-paid opinion

The danger in synthesis is the HiPPO, the highest-paid person's opinion deciding by status rather than evidence: even small signals from senior leaders may shape discussion, suppress dissent, and redirect resources before evidence receives full consideration (HBR, "The Antidote to HiPPOs"). The antidotes the skill builds in:

- **Independent input before discussion.** Each function forms its view in isolation (see `lenses.md`), so the room is not anchored before it starts.
- **Weight by relevance, not volume or status.** The chair weights each view by how much it bears on this decision and its criteria, not by how forcefully or how senior the voice. Input is time-boxed and carries no veto; only a predefined threshold (legal, risk) triggers an Agree or veto.
- **Surface the minority view.** The dissent is recorded by role, not averaged away (see `memo.md`).
- **Disagree and commit.** Once the call is made, the one decision binds the dissenters: state the objection, record it, and move as one (McKinsey; the Amazon practice).

## The chair speaks last

Anchoring is the mechanism to defeat: the member who speaks first often has an outsized effect on the final decision (BoardPro, "Cognitive Biases in Board Decision Making"). The discipline is explicit at Amazon: take the floor in order of seniority with the most junior first, and if you are the most senior person in the room, go last, because if the senior voice speaks first even strong, high-judgment people will wonder whether their own read is wrong (Fortune, on the Amazon meeting practice). So the chair solicits the independent functional views first, withholds their own preference, and states it last. This converges three findings into one rule: Janis's "leader stays impartial early," the HiPPO antidote, and the anchoring research.

## Why multi-perspective beats one view (the evidence)

The reason the skill convenes a board at all:

- **Cognitive diversity makes teams faster on hard problems.** Reynolds and Lewis ran a strategic-execution exercise more than 100 times and found a significant correlation between high cognitive diversity (differences in perspective and information-processing) and high performance; the fastest teams on new, uncertain problems were the most cognitively diverse (HBR, 2017).
- **Structured dissent raises decision quality.** Groups using dialectical inquiry and devil's advocacy made significantly higher-quality decisions than consensus groups, though consensus groups reported more satisfaction, the tradeoff to manage (Schweiger, Sandberg and Ragan, Academy of Management Journal). Even a wrong devil's advocate improves quality by forcing deliberate rather than automatic thinking.

## The failure mode: consensus collapsing into groupthink

Multi-perspective deliberation fails when the striving for agreement overrides the realistic appraisal of alternatives, which is groupthink (Irving Janis, 1972). Its symptoms cluster in three groups: overestimation (the illusion of invulnerability, belief in the group's morality), closed-mindedness (collective rationalization, stereotyping outsiders), and pressure toward uniformity (self-censorship, the illusion of unanimity, direct pressure on dissenters, self-appointed mindguards). Confirmation bias compounds it, the pull to seek and favor information that affirms what the room already believes (BoardPro).

Janis's antidotes, which the skill operationalizes: the leader assigns every member the role of critical evaluator; the leader stays impartial and withholds a preference early; a formal devil's advocate is appointed; independent subgroups work the same problem in parallel; and a second-chance meeting is held before final commitment. McKinsey adds the same prescription: formal processes, checklists, devil's advocates, and competing analytic teams shake up the debate. The skill's parallel independent views (Convene), its dissent pass (Challenge), and its chair-speaks-last rule (Decide) are these antidotes in operating form.
