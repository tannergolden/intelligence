# Founding playbook - the zero-to-one operating manual for found mode

*The gated idea-to-launch sequence for found mode: validate a real problem, build the smallest thing that learns, incorporate without tripping an irreversible gate, set founder equity, win the first customers and the first hire, and raise the pre-seed or seed. Read this on every found run, alongside lifecycle.md (stage), specialist-dispatch.md (how a lead frames and dispatches), and write-gate.md (what escalates to the Founder).*

Found mode protects two things above all: **runway** and **learning velocity**. A pre-revenue idea is not run like a company; it is run like a search for one. The job is not to execute a plan, it is to find a plan worth executing before the money runs out. So the dominant question at this stage is **"is there real evidence of product-market fit?"**, and revenue is a lagging vanity signal that proves nothing yet. The whole sequence below is gated: each stage ends in a go/no-go gate, and the standard call at every gate is **pivot, persevere, or kill**. Resist premature process and premature hiring; both burn the two things you are protecting.

This is a found-stage reference. The convened roster is small by design (lifecycle.md: found scales to the chair plus the one or two functions a step touches). The CEO chairs and holds the product call, each department a step touches is convened through its own skill (its lead frames and dispatches its specialists), the quality skill's ★ Head of Quality verifies anything load-bearing, and **board is the sole writer**: agents and the department skills compose and return, and board persists into the venture's record behind write-gate.md. Two gates here are irreversible and belong to the Founder alone: incorporation and the equity split. They are flagged in line, every time.

## The gated sequence (the spine)

Found runs as a sequence of stages, each closed by a go/no-go gate. Do not skip ahead: incorporating before you have evidence is how a founder ends up with a registered agent fee, a Delaware franchise tax bill, and an 83(b) clock ticking on a company that should have stayed an idea.

```
1. Problem & customer discovery   -> Gate: is the problem real, urgent, and ours to solve?
2. Lean canvas & riskiest assumption -> Gate: do we know the one thing that, if false, kills this?
3. Build-measure-learn / MVP        -> Gate: pivot, persevere, or kill (validated learning)
4. Incorporation [IRREVERSIBLE]     -> Gate: is the VC-track / lifestyle call made, entity chosen?
5. Founder equity & vesting [IRREVERSIBLE] -> Gate: split agreed, vesting set, 83(b) filed in 30 days
6. First go-to-market & first customers -> Gate: repeatable path to the next 10 customers?
7. The founding hire                -> Gate: is this the one role that unblocks the bottleneck?
8. Pre-seed / seed fundraise        -> Gate: is the round a means to a milestone, or a goal?
```

Each gate is a `decide`-shaped moment inside a `found` run: every angle the stage touches is heard, and the call resolves to one of pivot / persevere / kill / proceed, with the Founder as the decider on anything irreversible.

## Stage 1 - Problem and customer discovery (Steve Blank)

The first error is building before you know whom you are building for. Customer discovery (Steve Blank, *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*) inverts the default: you do not write a business plan and execute it, you **leave the building** and test whether the problem you imagine actually exists, for a customer who will actually pay. Blank's frame is that a startup is a temporary organization built to **search for a repeatable and scalable business model**, not a small version of a large company executing a known one.

The discipline:
- **Customer development runs parallel to product development**, not after it. You test the problem, the customer, and the willingness to pay before, and while, you build.
- **Get out of the building.** Conduct problem-validation interviews. Ask about the customer's world and their current workaround, not about your idea. The strongest signal is a problem the customer is already spending time or money to solve badly.
- **Hypotheses, then tests.** Every assumption about who the customer is, what they need, and what they will pay is a hypothesis to be falsified, not a fact to be defended.

Convene: the CEO chairs and frames the problem hypotheses (the discover skill widens them when they are thin), the research skill designs the problem-validation interviews and synthesizes the evidence, and the sales skill's ★ Chief Revenue Officer (with the Product Marketing Manager) frames the customer and the market. **Gate 1:** the problem is real (customers confirm it unprompted), urgent (they are already trying to solve it), and ours (we have an unfair reason to win). No evidence of urgency is a no-go: refine the problem or kill it. Record the validated and falsified hypotheses to the venture's Log.md; do not advance on a problem nobody confirmed.

## Stage 2 - The lean canvas and the riskiest-assumption test

Before any code, capture the model on one page. The **Lean Canvas** (Ash Maurya's adaptation of Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas, retuned for startups) replaces the comfortable boxes with the dangerous ones. Its nine blocks: **Problem** (top 3), **Customer Segments** (and early adopters), **Unique Value Proposition** (the single clear why-you message), **Solution** (top 3 features), **Channels** (path to customers), **Revenue Streams**, **Cost Structure**, **Key Metrics** (the few numbers that matter), and **Unfair Advantage** (what cannot be copied or bought). The canvas is a snapshot of bets, not a plan; it exists to be revised as you learn.

The canvas exists to surface the **riskiest assumption**: of all the things that must be true for this to work, which one, if false, kills it, and which one is least proven? That is what you test next, before anything else. The **riskiest-assumption test (RAT)** is the smallest experiment that gives a clear true/false read on that one bet. The RAT comes before the MVP: an MVP is a product, a RAT is often just a landing page, a concierge run done by hand, a smoke test, or ten pre-sale conversations. Build the MVP only after the riskiest assumption survives the cheap test.

Convene: the chair (who holds the product call), the research skill on what the evidence so far supports, and the analytics skill's ★ Chief Data Officer to name the key metric that will read the test. **Gate 2:** you can name the single riskiest assumption and the cheapest experiment that settles it. If the riskiest assumption is "customers will pay," the test is asking for money (a pre-order, a letter of intent, a deposit), not a survey. Board writes the canvas and the RAT design into the venture's strategic core (Administration/Strategy.md), per write-gate.md.

## Stage 3 - Build-measure-learn, the MVP, and validated learning (Eric Ries)

This is the engine of found mode: the **Build-Measure-Learn loop** (Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup*). You build the smallest thing that produces a measurable signal, measure how customers actually behave, and learn whether your hypothesis held. Then you decide. The loop's unit of progress is **validated learning**: evidence that a hypothesis about the business is true or false, earned by real customer behavior, not by shipped features or hours worked.

- **The MVP is the minimum version that completes one turn of the loop**, the least product that lets you learn the most. It is a learning tool, not a small product launch. Forms range from a landing page, a concierge MVP (you do it by hand behind the scenes), or a Wizard-of-Oz MVP (manual work that looks automated), up to a single working feature.
- **Measure with cohort and behavior data, not vanity metrics.** Total signups and cumulative revenue flatter and mislead; activation, cohort retention, and meaningful repeat usage tell the truth. Pair the MVP with the one key metric from the canvas.
- **Learn, then choose: pivot or persevere.** A **pivot** is a structured change to one element of the model (the customer, the problem, the channel, the revenue model) while keeping one foot in what you learned; a **persevere** doubles down on a validated path. The pivot-or-persevere call runs after each loop, and it is the most important recurring decision a founder makes.

Convene: the develop skill's ★ Engineering Lead builds the MVP or prototype, the research skill reads the customer behavior behind the numbers, the analytics skill's ★ Chief Data Officer reads the metric, and the CEO chairs and makes the pivot-or-persevere call, which is a product decision. The quality skill's ★ Head of Quality verifies that the metric actually measures the hypothesis (a vanity metric that confirms the founder's hope is the classic self-deception, see verification.md). **Gate 3** is the loop's own gate: **pivot, persevere, or kill**, grounded in the cohort data. Persevering on a hypothesis the data falsified is the cardinal found-stage sin. Each loop's call and its evidence are written to Log.md so the next loop is not blind.

> PMF signal, not proof: early signs of product-market fit are qualitative and behavioral, retention that flattens into a durable cohort, customers who would be "very disappointed" without the product (Sean Ellis's 40% threshold), pull you cannot keep up with, and word of mouth. Do not declare PMF off a single spike. Suppress Rule of 40, Magic Number, and burn multiple here; lifecycle.md tags them as not-yet-load-bearing until scale.

## Stage 4 - Incorporation [IRREVERSIBLE, FOUNDER GATE]

Incorporation is the first gate the skill cannot pass autonomously. Entity choice, jurisdiction, and timing have tax and fundraising consequences that are expensive or impossible to unwind, so the legal skill's ★ General Counsel (with the finance skill on the tax and the cap-table side) frames the options and **The Founder decides** (write-gate.md: changing the entity or the cap table is hard-gated). The board frames; the Founder signs.

The default rule:
- **Delaware C-corp when a VC track is realistic.** If the venture intends to raise institutional equity, the strong default is a Delaware C-corporation. Investors expect it: Delaware corporate law is the deepest and most predictable, the C-corp is the structure a priced round, a stock-option pool, and preferred stock all assume, and venture funds (with tax-exempt and foreign LPs) generally cannot hold pass-through interests. A C-corp also opens the **QSBS** door (Stage 5). The cost is **two layers of tax** (the corporation pays, then shareholders pay on dividends or sale) and annual formalities plus Delaware franchise tax.
- **LLC for a lifestyle or cash-flow venture.** If the goal is owner income rather than a venture exit, an LLC gives **pass-through taxation** (one layer, profits flow to the members' returns), far lighter formalities, and flexible ownership. It is the explicit-deviation path, chosen deliberately, not the default. An LLC can convert to a C-corp later, but the conversion is friction and can have tax cost, and it does not retroactively start the QSBS clock.
- **The choice is the VC-track question, made explicitly.** Do not default by inertia. The board states the realistic funding path, the Founder confirms it, and the entity follows from it.

Mechanics that ride along with incorporation: authorize enough shares (a common convention is 10,000,000 at founding) at a nominal par value, issue founder stock and **pay for it** (cash or assigned IP, so it is genuinely owned), get the EIN, open the bank account, and execute the founders' agreement, confidential-information-and-invention-assignment (CIIA) agreements, and the IP assignment so the company, not a founder personally, owns what was built. **Gate 4:** the funding-path call is made, the entity is chosen to match, and the formation pack is ready for the Founder's signature. The skill drafts; the Founder executes.

## Stage 5 - The hard irreversible clocks (83(b) and QSBS)

Two deadlines at founding are irreversible, unforgiving, and routinely missed. Surface both, in line, on every incorporation run. The skill flags them; the Founder and counsel act on them.

- **The 83(b) election, filed within 30 days [HARD DEADLINE].** When a founder receives stock subject to vesting, Section 83(b) of the Internal Revenue Code lets them elect to be taxed on the stock's value **now**, at grant, when it is near zero, instead of as it vests (when it may be worth far more, each vesting event becoming ordinary income on the spread). The election **must be filed with the IRS within 30 days of the stock grant**, full stop. There is no extension and effectively no remedy if missed: the founder faces ordinary-income tax on the appreciation as shares vest, and the capital-gains holding clock starts late. **Almost every founder with vested stock should file the 83(b).** This is the single most common irreversible founding mistake. The skill must surface the 30-day deadline the moment restricted stock with vesting is issued, and route the filing to the Founder and counsel; it is not an autonomous write.
- **The QSBS 5-year clock starts at issuance [LONG-HORIZON GATE].** Qualified Small Business Stock (IRC Section 1202) can exempt a large share of the gain on sale from federal capital-gains tax, a major founder and early-employee benefit, **but only if the stock is held for at least 5 years** and the company met the requirements at issuance: it must be a **domestic C-corporation** (LLCs do not qualify, one more reason the entity choice matters), with gross assets at or below the statutory threshold when the stock is issued, in a qualifying (non-excluded) trade or business, and the stock acquired at original issue. The clock starts the day the stock is issued. Choosing an LLC, or delaying the C-corp, delays or forfeits QSBS. Note the rules and thresholds were updated by 2025 legislation; confirm the current figures with counsel rather than quoting a stale number. The skill records the issuance date and the 5-year date so the venture's later sale decisions are not blind to it.

These two clocks are why incorporation is gated and why timing matters: the C-corp choice and the issuance date set the QSBS clock, and the 83(b) window opens and closes inside 30 days of that same issuance. Get them wrong and no later cleverness recovers the money.

## Stage 6 - Founder equity and vesting

The equity split is the second irreversible Founder gate and the most relationship-fragile decision in the company's life. Unwinding a bad split later means asking a cofounder to give back stock, which rarely ends well. The board frames the principles; the Founder and cofounders decide; the skill never sets equity autonomously (write-gate.md).

- **Split on future contribution and risk, not on the idea or the past.** The idea is worth little; execution over the years ahead is worth almost everything. Weigh who is full-time versus part-time, who took the salary cut, who carries which risk, and who will drive the value going forward. A roughly equal split among genuinely committed cofounders is common and often healthiest; a wildly lopsided split needs a real reason both sides accept.
- **Vesting protects everyone, including the founders.** The convention is **4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff**: nothing vests until 12 months (the cliff), then monthly or quarterly over the remaining three years. The point is that a cofounder who leaves in month 6 does not walk away with a quarter of the company. Vesting is standard, expected by investors, and protects the founders who stay.
- **Acceleration, and the founder-departure case.** Define single- and double-trigger acceleration on a change of control up front, and decide what happens to unvested shares if a founder leaves. These are cheap to agree now and bitter to negotiate later.
- **Reserve the option pool deliberately.** Carve out a stock-option pool (commonly 10 to 20 percent) for early employees, and understand that investors will usually require the pool to be set before their money goes in, so it dilutes the founders, not the new investor. Know this going into the round.

Convene: the legal skill's ★ General Counsel and the finance skill frame the cap-table mechanics and the vesting terms; the people skill's ★ Chief Human Resources Officer frames the option pool and early-hire equity; the Founder decides. **Gate 5 / 6:** the split is agreed and documented, vesting is set with the cliff, **and the 83(b) is filed within 30 days of the restricted-stock grant** (Stage 5). Board writes the cap table and vesting schedule into the venture's finance records (`Cap-Table.md`, per the finance skill) only after the Founder confirms.

## Stage 7 - First go-to-market and the first customers

Found-stage GTM is founder-led and unscalable on purpose. The startup rule applies: **do things that don't scale**. The founder sells, onboards, and supports the first customers by hand, because the goal is not efficiency, it is learning what makes a customer say yes and stay.

- **Founder-led sales.** The founder runs the first conversations and closes the first deals personally. No sales hire, no funnel automation yet, the founder is the funnel. Every objection, every reason a deal stalls, every feature a customer asks for is validated learning that no hire could surface as cleanly.
- **The first 10 customers over the first 1,000.** Recruit a small set of design-partner customers who feel the problem acutely and will tolerate a rough product in exchange for being heard. Make them absurdly happy. A handful of customers who love the product beats a crowd who merely like it; intensity of love, not breadth, is the PMF signal.
- **Find the repeatable thread.** The GTM gate is not "did we get customers," it is "do we know **why** they bought and **how to get the next ten the same way**." A pipeline of one-off wins with no common pattern is not a go-to-market, it is luck.

Convene: the sales skill's ★ Chief Revenue Officer (with the Product Marketing Manager on positioning and the Demand Generation Manager on demand) and the customers skill's ★ Chief Customer Officer frame the motion and the onboarding-to-first-value path; the CEO chairs. **Gate 6:** there is a repeatable path to the next 10 customers, with the why-they-buy understood. Channel, message, and conversion notes are written to the venture's GTM surface (Brand.md / Products.md).

## Stage 8 - The founding hire

The first hire is a high-leverage, high-cost decision, and the found-stage default is **to resist it** until a real constraint demands it. Premature hiring is one of the two found-stage runway killers. Hire when, and only when, the founders are the bottleneck on a specific, durable need, not to feel like a real company.

- **Hire against the binding constraint.** Name the one thing the founders cannot do, or cannot do fast enough, that is now limiting the company. The first hire fills that, whether it is the engineer who lets the technical founder stop firefighting, or the seller who lets the business founder stop selling. If you cannot name the constraint, you are not ready to hire.
- **Early hires are generalists who thrive without structure.** At this stage there is no process to onboard into; the right early employee is high-agency, comfortable with ambiguity, and able to own an outcome end to end. Culture-add and trajectory matter more than a perfect resume.
- **Compensate with meaningful equity from the pool.** Early employees take below-market cash for real equity (from the Stage 6 option pool) and the vesting that comes with it. Be honest about the risk and generous with the upside; the first hires take nearly founder-level risk.

Convene: the people skill's ★ Chief Human Resources Officer (with a talent acquisition seat spawned on demand) frames the role, the bar, and the package; the lead of the department the hire joins frames what the hire must be able to do; the Founder decides on the offer (an offer is an external commitment, write-gate.md). **Gate 7:** this hire unblocks a named bottleneck and the equity grant is set; if no bottleneck is named, the no-go is "do not hire yet." Equity grants land on the cap table only after the Founder confirms.

## Stage 9 - Pre-seed and seed fundraising basics

Raise money to hit a milestone, not because raising is the goal. Found-stage fundraising is about buying enough runway to reach the next proof point (PMF evidence, a revenue threshold, a metric that de-risks the next round) and nothing more. More money raised is more dilution and a higher bar for the next round; raise what the milestone needs.

- **Pre-seed vs seed.** **Pre-seed** funds the search: getting from idea to an MVP and the first signals, often from founder savings, friends and family, angels, or accelerators. **Seed** funds the proof: getting from early signal to demonstrable product-market fit, typically the first institutional or angel-led round. The line blurs, but the question each answers differs: pre-seed asks "can we build it and will anyone care," seed asks "is this PMF and can it scale."
- **The instrument: SAFE or convertible note.** Early rounds usually avoid setting a firm valuation by using a **SAFE** (Simple Agreement for Future Equity, the Y Combinator standard) or a **convertible note**. Both convert to equity at a later priced round, governed by a **valuation cap** (the maximum valuation at which the money converts, protecting the early investor) and sometimes a **discount**. A SAFE is not debt and has no maturity or interest; a convertible note is debt with a maturity date and interest, which adds pressure. Understand that **caps stack and dilute**: multiple SAFEs at different caps all convert at the priced round, and the founder feels the combined dilution then, not now. Model the as-converted cap table before signing, not after.
- **What pre-seed and seed investors actually buy.** At pre-seed, they buy the **team and the problem**; there is little else to underwrite. At seed, they buy **early evidence**: signs of PMF, a credible wedge, and a market large enough to matter. The pitch is a tight narrative (problem, why-now, the wedge, the team, the early traction, the ask and the milestone it buys), not a feature tour.
- **Runway and the milestone math.** The round should buy roughly 18 to 24 months of runway to a milestone that clearly de-risks the next raise. Know the burn, know the milestone, and size the round to reach it with margin. Closing a round is not progress; the milestone the round buys is.

Convene: the finance skill (its ★ Financial Planning Lead) frames the round size, dilution, and the runway-to-milestone math; the legal skill's ★ General Counsel frames the instrument and the cap-table effect; the chair (Chief Executive Officer), who holds strategy framing, frames the narrative; the Founder decides whether and how much to raise (taking money is an irreversible external commitment, write-gate.md). **Gate 8:** the round is sized to a named milestone, the instrument and cap are modeled as-converted, and the Founder authorizes the raise. The pitch narrative and the model are written to the venture's strategic and financial core; the actual signing is the Founder's act, never the skill's.

## How the playbook runs (the doctrine, restated for found)

- **Every angle heard, one owner accountable.** At each gate the functions the stage touches are heard in isolation, and the call resolves to one of pivot / persevere / kill / proceed, with one accountable owner. The chair holds the question and synthesizes; no function grades its own input (the metric that reads a test is verified by the Head of Quality, not by the founder who hoped for the result).
- **Stage-scaled and small.** Found convenes the smallest sufficient roster (lifecycle.md). A two-function call gets two functions, not the whole board; a board of eleven on a problem-validation question is wasted tokens and false weight.
- **Board is the sole writer.** Agents and the department skills compose the canvas, the model, the cap table, the pitch; board persists them into the venture's record behind write-gate.md, with any existing file's last state committed first and appending the gate decision to Log.md so the next found run is not blind.
- **The irreversible gates belong to the Founder.** Incorporation, the entity choice, the equity split, the 83(b) filing, taking investor money, and any external offer are hard-gated. The board frames the call to the highest quality it can; the Founder is the only one who signs. The 83(b) 30-day clock and the QSBS 5-year clock are surfaced in line, every time, because no later work recovers a missed one.
