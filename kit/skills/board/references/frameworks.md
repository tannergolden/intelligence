# Frameworks - the decision toolkit

A library of multi-perspective decision frameworks, grouped by the job each does. The chair reaches for the few a decision needs, names them in the brief, and never runs all of them. Each entry: what it is, and when to reach for it.

## A. Surface dissent and stress-test the plan

- **Devil's advocacy.** One member is assigned to argue against the leading proposal on purpose, to expose its flaws. A recognized antidote to groupthink: Irving Janis recommends rotating the role (a different person each time) and inviting outside challenge. Reach for it when the room is converging fast and nobody is voicing doubt. Caveat (Nemeth; Sunstein): assigned, inauthentic dissent is weaker than genuine disagreement and can even entrench the majority, so prefer a real objection or a dedicated team where you can.
- **Dialectical inquiry.** A structured debate of two genuine, fully developed positions, a plan and a credible counter-plan built on different assumptions, argued against each other; the decision is synthesized from the clash. Use on high-stakes, assumption-heavy calls where you want the underlying assumptions themselves tested, not just objections raised.
- **Red teaming.** An independent group plays the adversary and actually attacks the plan, then reports the vulnerabilities. Stronger than verbal objection because the challenge is real and external. Reach for it before a real threat: a competitive move, a security posture, a decision where complacency is the danger.
- **Pre-mortem (Gary Klein, HBR 2007).** Imagine the decision has already failed a year out, then work backward to generate the plausible reasons why. The prospective-hindsight framing gives people permission to voice the reservations they would otherwise suppress, and sharpens the ability to name what could go wrong. Run it during planning, before committing. The everyday, lightweight complement to a full red team.
- **Six Thinking Hats (Edward de Bono).** Run the whole room through one mode at a time: white (facts), red (feelings), black (caution), yellow (benefits), green (creativity), blue (process). A parallel-thinking protocol for running the deliberation itself, not a decision rule; it keeps the room in the same mode at the same time so the loudest register does not dominate. Reach for it when discussion keeps fragmenting or one register (usually caution) is swamping the rest.

## B. Assign decision rights

- **RAPID (Bain).** Five roles in a decision: Recommend (drives the analysis), Agree (must sign off, for example legal or risk), Perform (executes), Input (consulted, no veto), Decide (the single accountable decision-maker). Reach for it when a decision stalls because who actually decides is unclear, or when many stakeholders are involved. The workflow runs roughly Input, Recommend, Agree, Decide, Perform. The point is a single D: there should ideally be only one decision-maker per choice.
- **RACI.** A responsibility matrix mapping tasks to people: Responsible (does the work), Accountable (one owner who answers for it), Consulted (two-way input), Informed (one-way updates). Use to clarify execution roles on the work after the decision, distinct from RAPID, which governs the decision itself.

## C. Score and structure the options

- **Weighted decision matrix (Pugh method).** Score the options against explicit criteria, weight each criterion by importance, sum the weighted scores. Reach for it when comparing several concrete alternatives on multiple dimensions (vendors, investments, designs). Caveat: the scores are judgments, not measurements, and many minor criteria can swamp a true must-have, so keep the criteria few and weighted.
- **MECE issue trees (Barbara Minto, McKinsey).** Break the problem into branches that are Mutually Exclusive and Collectively Exhaustive, no overlaps and no gaps, paired with a leading hypothesis to test. Use early, to structure a messy decision into clean parts before any scoring, so the options are genuinely distinct and nothing is missed.

## D. Test against the future or an adversary

- **Scenario planning (Kahn; Wack at Shell).** Build a small set of distinct, plausible future narratives (typically 2 to 3) and test whether the decision holds across all of them, rather than betting on one forecast. Reach for it under deep, long-horizon uncertainty where a single forecast would be fragile.
- **War-gaming.** A simulation where participants play roles, including a thinking adversary, and adapt inside the scenario. Use when the outcome is shaped by a reactive opponent (a competitor, a negotiation), so responses must be tested interactively.
- **Vroom-Yetton-Jago.** A contingency tree for choosing how much to involve others, from autocratic to consultative to full group, via diagnostic questions on quality, information, and acceptance. Reach for it when the open question is process, not content: how participative should this particular decision be.
- **Cost-benefit analysis.** Monetize the costs and benefits, discount to present value, and compare on a common basis. Use for quantifiable trade-offs. Limits: it ignores distribution (who wins and loses), discounting undervalues the long term, and it cannot price non-economic values, so pair it with the lenses above.

## Picking a framework

Match the tool to the job, not the fashion. If the room is converging too fast, reach into Group A (dissent). If who-decides is unclear, Group B (rights). If the options need comparing, Group C (score). If the future is the risk, Group D. A standard run uses one Group A tool plus RAPID; a deep run layers a dissent battery, a scoring or dialectical pass, and a future test.
