# Deliberation - how the chair runs the room

How `board` runs a decision, and the governance that underwrites it. Read before framing a run. The chair (the CEO) owns the process; the standard is every angle heard, one owner accountable.

## The run, end to end

A board decision runs on a fixed rhythm: pre-read, framed discussion, then resolution. The chair owns that rhythm. Across the deliberation the chair convenes the meeting, sets the agenda, surfaces each participant's perspective, manages the discussion, and drives the room to a single decision with one accountable owner (Board-room.org, "Chairman of the Board Responsibilities"; OnBoard, "How to Run a Board Meeting").

The five phases the skill runs:

1. **Frame (the chair).** State the decision as one question at the right altitude: broad enough to admit real options, narrow enough to give traction, never a baked-in answer. Carry the situation, the complication, and the criteria the call will be judged on (SCQA, below). The chair (the CEO) lays out a MECE set of 2 to 3 genuine options. Restate the frame a few ways before any view is taken: a wrong frame decides the wrong question. Good board practice sends the framing and the pre-read far enough ahead that members arrive prepared, so the meeting is strategic rather than informational (AHA Trustee Services, "Deliberate Decision-Making").

2. **Convene (independent functional views).** Each relevant department lead, dispatched from its own department skill (`workforce.md`), forms its view in isolation, on the same framed question and criteria, before any discussion. This is the anti-anchoring discipline (see `synthesis.md`): independent input first, so the room is not steered by the first or the most senior voice.

3. **Challenge (structured dissent).** Before the call hardens, a devil's advocate argues the opposing case and a pre-mortem imagines the decision has failed. The chair "creates space for dissent, and builds consensus without steamrolling minority views" (Board-room.org). The frameworks are in `frameworks.md`.

4. **Decide (the chair, last).** The chair weighs the views by relevance, applies RAPID so one decider commits, names the one accountable owner, and states their own view last. Irreversible or major calls escalate to the Founder as the decider.

5. **Record (the Corporate Secretary).** The decision, its owner, the options, the risks, and the minority view are written to the memo (`memo.md`).

## The chair's job, specifically

The chair "guides the conversation, ensures that all directors have an opportunity to voice their perspectives, manages the allotted time for each topic, and steers the board toward clear decisions" (OnBoard). The hard parts:

- **Surface every perspective.** The chair makes sure every member is heard, particularly those who may not regularly speak up, so the call is not dominated by the confident few (AHA; OnBoard). This is why the skill takes each functional view independently and in parallel.
- **Protect dissent.** Effective chairs create space for dissent and build the call without steamrolling minority views (Board-room.org). Dissent is invited, not smoothed over.
- **Drive to one owner.** Discussion converges to a decision with a single accountable owner. Decision-rights models (RAPID, DACI) insist on exactly one decider: "the moment you have two Approvers who disagree, you are back to the exact problem the framework was supposed to prevent" (Atlassian, DACI). Contributors have a voice, not a vote.

## Governance basics (why the board is shaped this way)

The board is the governing body: it sets strategy, oversees management, and protects the owners' interests, while management runs day-to-day execution (Ideals, "Board of Directors vs Management"). The split creates checks and balances: the board holds strategic oversight; management retains daily control except on major strategic shifts. The board owes fiduciary duties: the duty of care (informed, diligent decisions), the duty of loyalty (the company's interest over personal), and the duty of obedience (alignment with mission and law) (ESOP Partners; NACD, "Fiduciary Duties of Corporate Directors").

In the company board runs, the hierarchy is explicit: the Founder sits above the CEO. So the board decides and names an owner for reversible, in-mandate calls, and escalates irreversible or major calls to the Founder as the decider, exactly as a management team escalates a board-reserved matter to its board. The line the board draws between what it decides and what it escalates is the same line a real board draws between management decisions and board-reserved matters (mergers, large irreversible investments, a change of direction).

## SCQA, the framing spine

Frame the opening of every decision as Situation, Complication, Question, Answer (Barbara Minto, the Pyramid Principle): the agreed Situation (context all sides share), the Complication (what changed, why now), the Question (the exact choice on the table), and the Answer (the recommendation, stated first). Communicate top-down, starting with the answer, then group the support (ModelThinkers, "Minto Pyramid / SCQA"). The frame is the chair's first product; the rest of the run fills it in.
